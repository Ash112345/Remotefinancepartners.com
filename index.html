It is not the people, the software or the trade

In a warehouse the barcode makes the record a by-product of the movement. The scan is not extra work, it is how the pick gets confirmed, so the record is created by the person doing the job at the moment they do it.

A wrapped pallet has no barcode. Bulk fuel is a quantity rather than an object. A cylinder swap is two unlabelled units changing hands at a kerb in the rain. So somebody writes down later what they think went out, and your accounts are built on top of that.

Someone is careless or dishonest.
The system is not good enough.
This trade is just like that and everyone lives with it.

Most owners arrive with one of those three theories. It is usually none of them. Telling an owner that his stock loss is a recording problem rather than a dishonest employee is the most useful thing we say in a first conversation, and it is true far more often than not.

Your team are solving problems in real time with the information they have. The business grew but the systems to manage it did not.

You already know the symptoms

  • The same delivery goes out twice. The first one never made it onto the system, so someone loads it again. You have paid for the stock, the driver and the fuel, twice, and the customer did not ask for it.
  • Stock moves between branches and nobody writes it down. One yard is short, so they pull from another to get the load out. Good instinct, right call on the day. The system never hears about it, and three weeks later a branch is down forty bags with nothing but an argument to explain it.
  • The count never agrees, and nobody can tell you whether it is theft, breakage, a mis-keyed delivery or a transfer that was never recorded.
  • Sales are worked out from the count rather than recorded independently, so stock and revenue can never check each other and no variance can ever arise.
  • Invoices go out days after the job, and it is felt as a cash flow problem rather than an admin one.
  • Every site writes up a delivery note its own way, and nobody has put the versions side by side.
  • You are still doing the numbers on a Sunday night.

People, process, platform. In that order.

People

Three people record the same delivery three different ways, one person just knows what is in the yard, and nobody is accountable for the numbers agreeing. We map who actually touches stock, cash and invoices, then give you a model that says who does what, who checks it, and who carries it if it is wrong.

Process

We trace transactions from order to delivery to invoice to bank, and find where information is re-keyed, delayed or lost. Every workaround your team has invented, we find, and either build it in properly or design it out. Then we write it down in plain language, two sides of A4 per process. If it does not fit on that, your team will not follow it.

Platform

Last, not first. Nine times out of ten the answer is the system you already own, configured properly. Delivery windows switched off. A stock module licensed and sitting idle. Mobile proof of delivery live on two sites out of ten. Where a gap is genuinely a tooling gap we will say so and tell you what it costs. Where it is not, we will tell you that too.

What good looks like

Every load recorded once, before it leaves. Branch transfers logged as they happen, on both sides. A delivery list you can trust on a Monday morning. Stock, cash and invoices that agree. A named owner against every check, and a routine your team runs without you standing over it.

None of that requires new software or a new hire. It requires the recording to happen where the work happens, rather than afterwards from memory.

What this looks like in practice

A ten yard fuel and solid fuel distributor. Stable, profitable, with a four person back office team, and a daily routine that only one of them could run.

What we found

  • Revenue was a residual rather than a record. Sales were inferred from a nightly count, so no variance could ever arise.
  • The spreadsheet column that checked closing stock was mathematically incapable of ever disagreeing with it.
  • Transfers between sites were put through as sales and returns, because no separate transfer route existed, so stock moving between two of the company's own yards looked identical to stock leaving the business.
  • Bulk out notes were being written in seven different formats across ten yards, so no rule could be applied to them and nothing could be checked automatically.
  • One rep, one round, one day: real trade sitting in the sales system, nothing at all in the sheet that drove revenue and banking.
  • A cash reconciliation ran correctly for months, stopped on a Thursday in July, and left three weeks of exceptions that nobody actioned.
  • 54 activities mapped across the daily stock and cash cycle. 15 with no owner at all. 12 resting on one individual.

The tolerances, the discount ceiling and the four steps for tracing a missing pallet all existed and all worked. They existed in one head.

What we did

People. One accountable owner for stock and cash accuracy. Cover for absence. Owners assigned against every one of the 54 activities, and the fourteen decisions only the Managing Director could make surfaced, put in front of him and closed.

Process. Our team learned the operation before changing any of it. We shadowed the daily routine end to end, sat with the people who record the movements, and traced transactions from order to delivery to invoice to bank.

What came out of that was a standard operating procedure the yards actually work to: one bulk out convention, one unit of account across the depot and the yards, a standard yard sheet, a daily cut-off, and the judgements written down alongside the steps rather than left in somebody's head.

Ten yards, standardised, without pretending they are identical. One yard exchanges empties on its own arrangement, one handles no pallets at all, one closes two days a week. Those are real differences and the procedure names them, so a variation is a documented exception rather than a site quietly doing its own thing. Standardising the way things are recorded is what lets the yards stay different where they need to be.

Platform. Configuration of what they already owned, and validation built into the sheets. No new software. We built a working file that records a transfer as a transfer, with both ends of the movement on it, because the system had no route for one. The file was the easy part. Getting both ends written up every time is what needs an owner.

What it achieves

The daily routine now runs to a written standard rather than from one person's memory, and more than one person can run it. Every yard is working to the same conventions, so a difference between two sites means something rather than nothing. Exceptions are logged, assigned and chased to closure instead of being noticed three weeks later. And the Managing Director has a monthly view of where the numbers are reliable and where they are not, which is the thing he did not have before.
